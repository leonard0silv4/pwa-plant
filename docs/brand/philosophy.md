# Herbarium Light

A sheet of pressed paper, a single specimen, one line of light passing over it. Herbarium Light treats the act of looking as the subject: the slow, patient attention of a botanist at a field desk, translated into form. Nothing shouts. The composition is a held breath — one organic shape set in generous, warm emptiness, measured with the care of someone who has drawn ten thousand leaves and kept only one.

Form is singular and asymmetric. Organic silhouettes are never mirrored perfectly; a leaf leans, its tip drifts a few degrees, its veins alternate rather than pair. Geometry appears only as the instrument of observation — a straight, thin horizontal band, perfectly level, the one rational gesture crossing a living form. The tension between the drawn and the measured is the entire idea, and it must be calibrated with painstaking precision so that neither dominates.

Color is drawn from material, not screens: deep moss ink, unbleached paper, the pale yellow-green of lichen on bark. Three tones, never more. The light band is the only luminous element, softened at its edges as if it were a reflection on glass rather than a laser. Contrast is quiet; legibility comes from shape, not saturation.

Space is the dominant material. Margins are wide and deliberate, the specimen sits slightly above optical center the way a mounted sample sits on a herbarium sheet. Scale moves between the monumental mark and whisper-thin annotation — catalogue numbers, coordinates, tiny mono labels — placed with master-level restraint so the eye always returns to the specimen.

Every line weight, curve tension and spacing interval should feel labored over: the product of deep expertise and countless refinements, crisp at 16 pixels and serene at 512. If something can be removed, it is removed. What remains must look inevitable.
